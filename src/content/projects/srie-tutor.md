<p class="lead">When people hand the routine to the machine, they lose the know-how and, with it, the judgment that would have caught its errors. The remedy proposed here is a tutor that measures what the learner knows before it answers.</p>

## The diagnosis

After four endoscopy centres adopted AI, adenoma detection fell six points on a skill built over years: 28.4% before AI, 22.4% once it was in routine use, and a missed polyp can mean an undiagnosed cancer. Where the machine runs the routine, practice tends to disappear and the skill goes blunt; recovery is partial even when the skill is retained (Bainbridge, 1983). Cognitive surrender names the general case: any task with cognitive friction is handed to the tool, and beyond the skill, a metacognitive decline follows, a reduced ability to learn. Despite the human cost, trust shifts to the tools that deliver productivity.

The loss falls on the young now and on a national economy later. Employment for young workers in AI-exposed roles sits 19% below their peers. Students with AI solved 48% more problems, then scored 17% worse closed-book: an illusion of mastery, a measured collapse. Shared algorithms cut cognitive diversity, so errors correlate even when each is better, and in the short term the damage does not show up in productivity; by the time it does, rebuilding would cost far more.

## The remedy: knowledge spaces, read by a language model

Bastani et al. ran a second arm of the same experiment, and the cost disappeared: with a trained AI supervisor, training performance rose 127% and exams matched the control group. A theoretical remedy already existed in knowledge spaces (Doignon and Falmagne, 1985), which describe a domain's items and the order in which they must be learned, from which every state a learner can occupy is derived. Cost kept them out of real settings: elicitation can run to a million queries, above all when building the space, and marking takes hours. A language model removes that cost, and where one teacher reaches thirty students, a tool reaches many more.

<dl class="work">
<dt>Modelling the structure</dt><dd>The domain's classical structure must be extended to fold in the metacognitive part. What belongs to the state, and how each part is identified, is genuinely open.</dd>
<dt>Automating the construction</dt><dd>Automatic domain construction counts as feasible if it matches what an expert would produce: the elicitation runs unchanged, with the model in the expert's place, on domains whose prerequisite order can be checked by hand.</dd>
<dt>Measuring a state</dt><dd>The LLM has to read a state well, fitting parameters and handling uncertainty. With no real students on this timeline, LLM personas stand in; success is recovering the state from answers and confidence alone.</dd>
<dt>Adaptive strategy</dt><dd>The tutor picks the next question by information gain over the estimated state, in the line of POMDP planning for teaching, with desirable difficulties kept in.</dd>
</dl>

<aside class="callout">
Education is only the nearest case. Personalisation is one instance of a general problem: wherever a formal object is too costly for a human to steer, a language model can become the hand that works it, with the structure saying where to look and the model carrying the language. Education receives one to two per cent of AI venture capital, and the scaffolding has stayed at pilot scale: the one tutor that raised training performance by 127% ran on prompt engineering alone.
</aside>
