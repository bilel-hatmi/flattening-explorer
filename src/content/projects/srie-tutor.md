<p class="lead">Cognitive surrender erodes both the know-how and the judgment that would have caught the error. The remedy proposed here is a tutor that measures what the learner knows before it answers.</p>

## The diagnosis

Four endoscopy centres adopted AI. Adenoma detection fell six points, on a skill built over years: 28.4% before AI, 22.4% after routine adoption, and a missed polyp can mean an undiagnosed cancer. Where the machine runs the routine, practice disappears and the skill goes blunt; recovery is partial even when the skill is retained. Cognitive surrender names the general case: any task with cognitive friction is handed to the tool, and beyond the skill, a metacognitive decline follows, a reduced ability to learn. Despite the human cost, trust shifts to the tools that deliver productivity.

The loss falls on the young now and on a national economy later. Employment for young workers in AI-exposed roles sits 19% below their peers. Students with AI solved 48% more problems, then scored 17% worse closed-book: an illusion of mastery, a measured collapse. Shared algorithms cut cognitive diversity, so errors correlate even when each is better, and in the short term the damage never shows up in productivity; by the time it does, rebuilding costs far more.

## The tool of the problem is the tool of the remedy

Bastani et al. ran a second arm of the same experiment, and the cost disappeared: with a trained AI supervisor, training performance rose 127% and exams matched the control group. A theoretical remedy already existed. Knowledge spaces (Doignon and Falmagne, 1985) describe a domain's items and the order in which they must be learned, from which every state a learner can occupy is derived. What kept them out of real settings was cost: elicitation can run to a million queries, above all when building the space, and marking takes hours. A language model removes that cost, and one teacher can only reach thirty students where a tool scales without limit.

<dl class="work">
<dt>Modelling the structure</dt><dd>The domain's classical structure must be extended to fold in the metacognitive part. What belongs to the state, and how each part is identified, is genuinely open.</dd>
<dt>Automating the construction</dt><dd>Whether automatic domain construction is feasible, judged against what an expert would produce: the elicitation runs unchanged, with the model in the expert's place, on domains whose prerequisite order can be checked by hand.</dd>
<dt>Measuring a state</dt><dd>Whether the LLM can read a state well, fitting parameters and handling uncertainty. With no real students on this timeline, LLM personas stand in; success is recovering the state from answers and confidence alone.</dd>
<dt>Adaptive strategy</dt><dd>Which question next: a policy driven by information gain over the estimated state, in the line of POMDP planning for teaching, with desirable difficulties kept in.</dd>
</dl>

<aside class="callout">
<strong>Beyond education.</strong> Personalisation is the near case of a general problem: wherever a formal object is too costly for a human to steer, a language model can become the hand that works it, with the structure saying where to look and the model carrying the language. Education earns no category of its own, one to two per cent of AI venture capital, and nobody has scaled the scaffolding: the one tutor that raised training performance by 127% ran on prompt engineering alone and was never scaled.
</aside>
