<p class="lead">Selected as a mentee on the SRIE programme, I was encouraged to lead an unofficial stream, in which I proposed three projects. Nobody asked for it and no course required it. I invested myself full time and now supervise seven Cambridge mathematics undergraduates on these topics.</p>

SRIE runs eight-week research placements in AI safety for Cambridge mathematics undergraduates, from 4 August to 28 September 2026. The three projects share a method more than a topic. Each starts from a construct that is usually discussed loosely (sycophancy, neutrality, understanding) and asks what it would take to measure it. The week before the projects began was a training I gave on a single point: the conditions under which a trait measured in a language model is valid at all.

Since the projects were ill-defined at the start, they had to remain accessible to second-year students, the students had to keep the freedom to dig into what interested them, and it was my first time supervising at this scale, on subjects I did not fully master. Having to be both reassuring and scientifically honest taught me to face not knowing, or knowing inexactly, more calmly, while keeping a reasoned dialogue.

## How the stream runs

Three levels, each carrying a different register of work, and no level does the job of another. The whole cohort meets about once a week for methodological sessions built on practical cases: literature review, reading and writing a paper, turning a qualitative question into a valid statistical design, and criticising an experiment while reading it. Each project group meets once or twice a week to present findings, think through the project's strategy and decide which way it goes; no student owns a whole project, each works on a complementary stream. One-to-one sessions run weekly on the individual plan.

Four things get built alongside the research, and they are on the timetable like everything else: method, tools (Claude Code across the whole range, from literature to code; Jira, Drive and Slack for the group), communication (presenting technical work in a structured way, asking the right question at the right moment), and the student's own way of working. Four sprints structure the eight weeks: a fixed training week, a literature review, an implementation phase reviewed by an external member of the AI safety mathematics community before the empirical work begins, and interpretation.

Today the projects are well formulated and the students fully invested; the first one produces a preprint at the end of September.
